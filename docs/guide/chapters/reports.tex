\section{Define the reader's question and knowledge}

\begin{headline}
  A report is an explanation for a named reader, built around the question that reader
  needs to answer. Name the question and the reader's knowledge before drafting.
\end{headline}

    transformation, and what remains untested?'' \\
    \rowcolor{ctpSurface0!30}
    Decision or use & The acceptance argument for the rewrite. \\
    Available evidence & Stage contract, source revisions, baseline and candidate artifacts,

    \rowcolor{ctpSurface0!30}

The report explains the acceptance argument; it need not reproduce every execution log.
The session's \code{METHODOLOGY_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md} (or the equivalent in your project)
connects terminology, mathematics, coursework, literature and unfamiliar methods to the
planned explanation.

\contract\ For the example reader:
\begin{itemize}
  \item explain any unfamiliar comparison method before using it to accept or reject agreement;
  \item prefer methods already grounded in the reader's coursework or mapped sources;
  \item give a custom diagnostic its motivation, definition, limitation and status. An unfamiliar
        method does not become self-explanatory merely because it produces a plot \cite[Design
          the argument]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Inspect the whole artifact and choose its form}

\begin{headline}
  Read the complete existing report before editing, then choose the lightest change and the
  lightest form that give the reader a clear route.
\end{headline}

For an existing report, read its complete source, outline, generated evidence and
rendered PDF first. Repetition, appended findings and chronology crowding out current
conclusions are reasons to reconsider the structure (Table~\ref{tab:reports-change})

Before drafting, assign each piece of material a role. In our example:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The opening states the question, bounded coverage, current finding and review status.
  \item The method explains the frozen contract and how the comparisons test it.
  \item The evidence follows stage order and makes differences inspectable.
  \item Interpretation explains what the evidence supports and what it cannot.
  \item An appendix preserves exhaustive comparisons, commands and provenance needed to reproduce
        the check.
\end{enumerate}
This is an example outline. The canonical workflow requires a coherent argument, not these
exact headings \cite[Design the argument]{skill:report-production}.

\section{Make every important claim independently checkable}

\begin{headline}
  The bibliography is part of the reader's working apparatus: the reader identifies a
  source, locates the passage and returns to the report's use of it.
\end{headline}

New substantial reports and substantial rewrites therefore use shared BibTeX-format
\code{.bib} databases, \code{biblatex} with Biber, linked citations and bibliography
back-references \cite[Bibliography and reader-verifiable
  pinpoints]{skill:report-production}.

\textbf{Keep the shared library in one directory}
\cite[Register the source you actually inspected]{skill:library}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item external literature has one owner in one file; maintained project references use a
        second; dated evidence is grouped by research topic in further files;
  \item each source has one stable key and the same descriptive short name across reports;
  \item a study entry may identify a defined evidence collection: the citation names the
        particular member record, while its manifest retains input identities and hashes;
  \item a rerun must not silently replace the version used as evidence by an earlier report.
\end{itemize}

Three pieces of information do different jobs (Figure~\ref{fig:reports-route}). The same
distinctions apply to the report's tables and captions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source identity}, in the bibliography: checked author or organization, title,
        edition or version, date, stable identifier where available, and access restriction. A
        repository source needs its exact relative path, owning role, and inspected revision or
        date.
  \item \textbf{Claim pinpoint}, in the citation beside the statement: the source's printed
        page, section, equation, table, figure or record supporting that claim.
  \item \textbf{Return route}: the bibliography's ``cited on'' pages lead back to this
        report's uses of the source. They are report pages, not source pinpoints.
\end{itemize}

A citation names the source that owns the claim (Table~\ref{tab:reports-hierarchy}).

\begin{rulebox}[Session records are not evidence]
  Brief, state, result, review and worker records never substantiate a measured or
  implemented fact.
\end{rulebox}

Citation and cross-reference links are blue (\code{colorlinks} with \code{ctpBlue}),
never hidden. Reports use descriptive names instead of labels borrowed from other
projects \cite[Source traceability and corroboration; Source hierarchy; Bibliography and
  reader-verifiable pinpoints; Writing character]{skill:report-production}.

\hook{reports-sources}

\paragraph{Pinpoints.} The following is citation syntax, with placeholder keys and locators
to replace after inspecting the actual sources:
\begin{lstlisting}
\cite[p.~23, sec.~3.9]{source-key}
\cites[pp.~10--11]{first-key}[sec.~2]{second-key}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each source in a grouped citation receives its own pinpoint.
  \item A bibliography entry's \code{pages} field usually describes the publication's extent or
        article number; it does not identify the passage supporting every claim.
  \item For a paper, check the printed pagination of the cited edition. For Markdown, cite the
        exact section heading.
  \item If a source is unavailable, mark the missing check rather than guessing a page
        \cite[Bibliography and reader-verifiable pinpoints]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{In the comparison example}, cite the stage record for the agreed
transformation, the precise source revision for implementation, and the comparison
artifact for observed agreement or disagreement. A workflow citation can justify the order
of checks; it cannot substantiate a measured result. A dirty working copy must be
identified as such rather than represented as the published base commit \cite[Source
  traceability and corroboration; Bibliography and reader-verifiable
  pinpoints]{skill:report-production}.

\paragraph{Figures.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item For a source PDF panel, record the caption's printed page and figure identifier
        separately from the extraction command's \code{page=} index; front matter can make these
        numbers differ.
  \item For a derived figure, identify both the input evidence and the method when they have
        different owners.
  \item Preserve access restrictions when reproducing material \cite[Choose form by information
          structure; Bibliography and reader-verifiable pinpoints]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Keep the editable source readable}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Format authored LaTeX with \code{latexindent} and the project's \code{latexindent.yaml}:
        two-space indentation and approximately 90-character prose lines. Preserve paragraphs,
        deliberate equation and table breaks, and code listings.
  \item Check formatting before handback with the report-writing skill's
        \code{check_report_sources.py}; its \code{--write} option applies the formatting.
  \item Generated tables acquire readable source through their generator, so the next build
        retains it.
\end{itemize}

Use \code{siunitx} for numerical results, quantities and units:
\begin{lstlisting}
\SI{25}{\giga\electronvolt}
\num{3084925}
\SI{0.0193}{\percent}
\unit{\pico\barn}
\SIrange{20}{100}{\giga\electronvolt}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Use \code{\SI} rather than \code{\qty}, which the \code{physics} package uses for
        delimiter sizing.
  \item Keep the original precision and uncertainty notation. Dataset identifiers, hashes,
        software versions, dates and algebraic constants retain their own notation.
  \item Numeric table columns can use \code{S} alignment when it helps comparison.
  \item The quantity scan flags likely omissions; checking the prose, captions and tables still
        requires distinguishing values from identifiers \cite[Readable source and numerical
          presentation]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Build the explanation around useful evidence}

\begin{headline}
  Build the aids first, then write the prose that presents the argument and connects them.
  If the reader would need to re-read a paragraph to follow it, it should become an aid.
\end{headline}

Introduce a table or figure before asking the reader to interpret it: state what it
compares, what is held fixed, and which feature answers the question. For the rewrite
example, a compact stage-comparison table can expose the first mismatch; a labelled
distribution comparison can show its shape. Exhaustive per-file logs belong in supporting
evidence unless a particular entry explains the conclusion \cite[Design the argument;
  Choose form by information structure]{skill:report-production}.

For a personal working report the target is a short time to read and digest, not a short
page count; note style is fine while it stays clear. Figure~\ref{fig:reports-forms} is a
recommended order of preference, which you may reorder for your readers.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A code or pseudo-code snippet may explain what code does when it is clearer than prose.
  \item A diagram explains a relationship; it does not by itself establish numerical agreement.
  \item A diagram that the report, plotbook or a talk share lives in its own file,
        \code{assets/<name>.tex}, with its numbers from macros the analysis writes, so all of
        them input the same picture \cite[Diagrams as asset files]{skill:report-production}.
  \item Keep source data separate from presentation when generating repeated tables, so prose and
        LaTeX do not acquire independently maintained copies of the same facts.
\end{itemize}

three (Table~\ref{tab:reports-devices}).

When the story jumps, reorganize: a definition or equation sits next to its first use.
Once a question is settled, a new dated revision may retire the presentation that served
it; earlier revisions keep the history \cite[Inspect before
  editing]{skill:report-production}.

\paragraph{Visual conventions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item portrait pages, readable spacing, explicit reading routes, restrained semantic colour and
        legible code blocks;
  \item every colour has a stable labelled meaning. A green ``evidence'' heading can identify an
        observed record, but a proposed expectation must not look like an already verified
        result;
  \item inspect grayscale rendering when colour carries meaning \cite[Colour
          convention]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Iterate figures and the plotbook before finishing the companion}

\begin{headline}
  Settle the plotbook's comparisons and page map before final prose. Figure and page
  design are two connected loops, each judged on the rendered result.
\end{headline}

\paragraph{Start from the equation.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each plotted quantity is an equation, numbered in the report or written in the plot's
        how-to-read note. The plot shows it as a function or its terms as histograms, with the
        equation's symbols in the labels.
  \item The standard grammar of the field comes first: a distribution with extra panels for a
        ratio, a pull or a diagnostic such as the stacked terms of a bin decomposition. A new
        plot type makes the reader learn a new grammar, so it must earn its place, and its note
        says why.
  \item Every axis is labelled: a shared $x$ axis once, under the lowest panel, and each panel
        its own $y$ axis. A mathematical expression is preferred to words unless a name reads
        better.
  \item If you maintain your own plotting library, add a missing reusable capability there, with
        a test, rather than drawing around it in a recipe \cite[Design and iterate at two scales;
          Reuse the implementation]{skill:plot-production}.
\end{itemize}

Begin with the reader's task and the evidence needed to perform it. Coverage and page
count are separate: a dense plotbook makes useful comparisons easy without discarding
unique measurements. Recover the applicable binning, statistical conventions and plotting
machinery, then plan candidate groupings. The first plan cannot establish how a
complicated overlay will read before it is rendered \cite[Design and iterate at two
  scales; Reuse the implementation]{skill:plot-production}.

      scales]{skill:plot-production}.}
  \label{fig:reports-loops}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Figure.} Colours, line styles and markers are tools. Keep them when they help
        decoding; revise them when they disappear or compete at final size.
  \item \textbf{Plotbook.} Decide what rows and columns mean. Bring alternatives together when
        simultaneous viewing helps; separate them when they obscure one another. A sparse page
        with tiny plots calls for reconsidering the composition.
  \item Neither a fixed grid nor a historical source/flavour split is a universal solution.
  \item Agents normally iterate within the confirmed contract. An unresolved scientific choice or
        a conflicting preference is settled by a recorded default that the owner reviews later,
        unless no default is defensible.
\end{itemize}

A recommended habit is to read the plotbook before the report, for a first impression, so
that the report's interpretation does not anchor the reading (the Einstellung effect).
The plotbook must therefore be readable on its own \cite[What a plotbook holds;
  Established appearance]{skill:plot-production}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item legends and colour keys sit inside the plot, and axis labels name the compared stages,
        for example $(N_\mathrm{BM}-N_\mathrm{BH})/N_\mathrm{BH}$, instead of a title or an
        explanation block atop the page;
  \item a plot whose reading is not obvious carries a short how-to-read note pointing to the
        report section or numbered equation that explains it, and no task-specific term is left
        undefined;
  \item the plotbook holds plots: a long table such as a per-event longtable does not belong, a
        small selected table may;
  \item a page with a large empty area is rejected: the render gate fails a blank band taller
        than \SI{25}{\percent} of the page height unless an exception is recorded;
  \item once results transfer between validation cohorts, the development cohort and one
        transfer-check summary replace side-by-side cohorts.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Final geometry.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prove the densest or least certain group in its actual page placement.
  \item Audit scale choices across every affected family, including tails, zero/signed bins and
        the uncertainty envelopes actually drawn.
  \item Reserve annotation space after overlays and final scales: a later axis-limit change can
        undo earlier clearance.
  \item Numerical preservation and geometry checks support this work but cannot judge whether the
        comparison communicates its effect \cite[Scales and final geometry; Render and
          accept]{skill:plot-production}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The companion report} follows Figure~\ref{fig:reports-companion}. Explain why
each comparison answers the question; assume domain knowledge, not participation in the
agent sessions. Introduce a population before its ratio and use a short worked example
where it removes a difficult conceptual step. Give recurring conventions one home
\cite[Write the companion after settling the plotbook]{skill:report-production}.

After the complete draft exists, read it as one argument:
\begin{itemize}
  \item remove repeated context;
  \item reconcile old and new conclusions;
  \item move chronology out of the main explanation;
  \item define terms before use;
  \item if the comparison has changed the conclusion, revise the opening and interpretation
        together rather than appending a corrective paragraph at the end \cite[Design the
          argument; Writing character]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Use separate review tracks and bounded corrections}

\begin{headline}
  A report can be grammatical and wrong, numerically sound and unsupported by its
  citations, or correct in source but unreadable in the PDF. Each track checks one of these.
\end{headline}

Internal review prepares the artifact for the owner's own inspection; it does not replace
their judgment (Table~\ref{tab:reviews}).

\paragraph{Scope each round.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item During drafting, review changed claims and their immediate dependencies.
  \item Once bounded findings are resolved, a substantial report needs the complete source
        corroboration and copy gates; apply the complete council review when physics or numerical
        conclusions require it \cite[Rounds]{skill:council}.
  \item After a local correction, repeat only the affected checks unless it changes structure,
        argument order, source hierarchy, global terminology, document-wide conventions, or
        claims outside that scope \cite[Source traceability and corroboration; Tiered
          review]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Coverage without repetition.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Complete coverage can combine prior accepted evidence and bounded rechecks; it need not
        repeat the same work in every reviewer session.
  \item Programmatic checks cover geometry, blank bands, printed values, inventory and navigation
        where applicable. Missing checks are not assessed, and an unblocked lint result alone is
        not readiness.
  \item Before visual dispatch, validate the explicit model and page selection with the render
        gate's preflight. Use an economical image-capable model for remaining reading tasks.
  \item Repagination reopens navigation and composition; it does not automatically invalidate
        accepted page-body readability.
  \item Expensive or exhaustive review requires a concrete unresolved problem \cite[Tiered
          review]{skill:report-production}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{rulebox}[Visual review]
  Never hand the visual reviewer a PDF.
\end{rulebox}

A vision report is evidence about appearance and needs comparison with the source and
intended rendering before it becomes a claim about correctness.

Give the independent reviewer the reader brief and artifact before the author's verdict.
It may find a structural problem even when the sentences are correct. Ask it to
\cite[Review packet; Figure and page tasks; Companion comprehension
  tasks]{skill:visual-review}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item reconstruct the question, findings and supporting comparisons without the session
        narrative;
  \item on rendered pages, trace a series, read the labels and identify the intended effect.
\end{enumerate}
Contact sheets establish overview coverage; individual pages at reading size are needed for
decoding and small text. An unreadable review image means that feature is not assessed.

\textbf{Findings} record the exact artifact, page, observation, reader impact and
recheck result. Verify alleged defects against the image: agreement with criticism does
not prove inspection. Iterate until reading tasks work and material defects are resolved;
neither a fixed number of passes nor repeated cosmetic changes establish quality
\cite[Findings and revision]{skill:visual-review}.

\section{Build, inspect, and deliver the exact PDF}

\begin{headline}
  A clean build proves that the source can be rendered. The source, copy, domain and visual
  reviews supply the rest of the evidence needed for delivery.
\end{headline}

The recommended bibliography setup uses \code{backend=biber}, \code{style=alphabetic},
\code{sorting=nyt} and \code{backref=true}, with readable, unambiguous short source
labels where they help. A manual build sequence is shown below; \code{latexmk} runs the
same steps and repeats them until the references settle:
\begin{lstlisting}
pdflatex -interaction=nonstopmode -halt-on-error report.tex
biber report
pdflatex -interaction=nonstopmode -halt-on-error report.tex
pdflatex -interaction=nonstopmode -halt-on-error report.tex
\end{lstlisting}
Inspect both logs and the rendered PDF \cite[Bibliography and reader-verifiable
  pinpoints]{skill:report-production}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item missing or duplicate keys, malformed data, unresolved references, rerun requests and
        material layout warnings;
  \item the rendered citation-to-entry links and bibliography return links;
  \item page appearance.
\end{itemize}

The authored \code{.tex} and \code{.bib} are the reconstructible source. The PDF and
ordinary bibliography/build intermediates remain generated \cite[Acceptance checklist;
  Bibliography and reader-verifiable pinpoints]{skill:report-production}.

\textbf{Hand back} \cite[Review and release]{skill:report-production}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the exact PDF and source paths, review evidence and remaining limits;
  \item for a requested plotbook companion, the matched pair with checked routes; raw logs remain
        supporting material;
  \item reviewable copies in one collection, delivered as soon as they are ready: a same-day
        revision replaces its file, a later date goes alongside, with every revision's hashes and
        human-review state in a manifest.
\end{itemize}
